Tropics Scorecard · Singapore · Feb 2024 to Oct 2026

How close was the forecast to what the instruments measured?

If a weather model is meant to learn from observations, it should be judged on them too. This is a small, working version of that judge: GFS and ECMWF forecasts scored against rain gauges, weather stations and airports around Singapore, with simple baselines and a 95 percent interval on every comparison.

Anthony Meijer · November 2026 · Code · Full dashboard

In short
  1. At the surface in the tropics, operational forecasts barely beat a free guess. No model is detectably better than climatology for temperature or wind at a station. For rain, ECMWF gains about 12 percent.
  2. The events that matter most are where they miss. A squall line put 10 mm in an hour on half the island's gauges, and no forecast had it at any lead time.
  3. The judge needs as much care as the model. One matching choice moves a score as much as switching models, and two of my first "wins" were faults in the forecast archive.
01 · The gap at the surface

Against the instruments, the forecasts barely beat a free guess.

Climatology is the forecast you get for free: what usually happens at this station, in this month, at this hour. For temperature, no model is detectably better than it, and three of the four are worse. For wind, all four are worse, and all read too windy. Only rain shows a real gain.

Part of this is a point being compared with a grid cell 9 to 28 km wide, part of it sea. That is the point: the gap between what a model says about a cell and what an instrument measures on the ground is real, measurable, and the space a model trained on the observations themselves would have to close.

Difference in mean absolute error against climatology on the same cases, one day ahead. Left of zero is better than climatology, right is worse. Bars are 95 percent intervals; a bar that crosses zero is no detectable difference.

02 · Rain

For rain the gain is real and holds out to seven days. It is also small.

ECMWF's rain is closer to the gauges than climatology at every lead time out to a week. GFS, one day ahead, is not detectably better than assuming tomorrow repeats today.

ECMWF publishes rain in 3-hour and 6-hour steps, so an hour-by-hour test is unfair to it. Switch to 6-hour totals and its gain over climatology holds, while the noise of a single hour drops out.

03 · Temperature

The models miss the city's daytime heat.

Both models run cool, and ECMWF runs coolest between 9 in the morning and 3 in the afternoon. On its grid the cell that holds most of Singapore is mostly sea, and sea does not heat up during the day the way a city does. A known physical cause, visible in the error by hour, is exactly what a single average would hide.

Mean error (forecast minus station) by local hour, one day ahead, in °C. Zero would be no bias. Shaded: 95 percent interval. Climatology sits 0.3 °C low because the test period was warmer than 2017 to 2023.

04 · Extremes

The events that matter are where the forecasts fail.

Rare, high-impact weather is the reason to forecast at all, and it is where a model that learned the average is weakest. I chose three events from the gauge data and public reports before looking at any forecast. The line marked Gauges is what was measured, as a network mean per hour; the others are what each model said one day ahead.

05 · How you judge

One matching choice moves the score as much as switching models.

A station has to be paired with one cell of the model's grid. Take the cell it sits in, or the nearest cell the model treats as land? For ECMWF the land rule moves 53 of Singapore's 54 southern stations to a cell centred in Johor, in another country.

I chose the cell the station sits in before seeing any score, and report the other as a check. A comparison that doesn't say which rule it used can't be compared with anyone else's.

06 · The instruments

Before scoring a forecast, I had to trust the instruments.

A model trained on raw observations sees every fault in them unless something catches it first. These are the ones the checks caught in 202 million readings. Each flag is logged with the check that raised it, and the main scores are computed both with and without the flagged data.

Stuck gauges

1,850 gauge-hours

A gauge read zero while the gauges within 10 km had a median of 5 mm or more: a blocked funnel or a dead logger.

Frozen sensors

25,843 readings

Temperature and rain sensors repeating the same reading for an hour or more, caught as flatlines and removed.

Clocks and siting

5 station-months

The warmest hour of the day fell at 10:00 or 18:00 instead of early afternoon: a shifted clock or a badly placed sensor.

Different buckets

19 of 63 gauges

tip at 0.197 to 0.204 mm, not 0.2. The rain threshold sits at 0.1 mm so a single tip counts on every gauge.

The feed itself

18,069 of 18,069

Before switching to a faster download endpoint, I compared it with the official one on a test day, reading by reading. Every shared rain reading matched.

A check against the record

18 of 18 days

The gauge data alone found Singapore's 2024 dry spell on exactly the dates in the official record.

07 · The archive

Two of my first wins were faults in the archive.

On the first run, GFS rain looked better seven days ahead than one day ahead. That cannot be right, so I looked. Beyond 120 hours the archived GFS rain is a third of its day-one amount, and forecasting a third of the rain lowers the error. I removed those lead times and wrote down why, even though it deleted a result that looked good. The ECMWF time steps in section 02 were the second fault.

A model trained on raw observations meets the same risk at scale: learning the instrument instead of the atmosphere. The checks have to be part of the pipeline, not an afterthought.

Mean forecast rain per hour over Singapore, by how far ahead the forecast was made. A model's average rain should not depend on lead time. ECMWF's holds steady; GFS drops by two thirds after day 3.

08 · The harness

Built to be reused: any forecast plugs in the same way.

One interface

Anything that forecasts answers the same question: these stations, these times, these lead times. Persistence, climatology and four operational models all use it, and a new model is one class.

One scorer

Every score, interval and comparison goes through one tested set of metric functions, so no two teams can compute "error" two different ways.

Rules before results

Matching, thresholds and baselines live in one config and a written protocol with a dated change log. The scorer refuses to run until the rules are signed off.

One command

One command rebuilds every table, score and chart from the raw downloads. Over 80 tests cover the metrics, matching and scorer.

09 · Next

What this judge would test next.

A model trained on observations

Scored at the same points, against the same baselines and intervals, so it can sit directly beside the operational models here.

Ensembles, scored as distributions

Ensemble forecasts, such as GenCast's, scored at each station with the continuous ranked probability score and reliability diagrams. Both scores are already in the code.

Holdouts that have never been seen

Whole stations and whole instruments held out, not only later dates, so nothing in the test has reached training in any form.

Beyond the record

Error tracked against how far an event exceeds anything in the training years, where learned models have been shown to struggle.

Scored by the decision

Not only how wrong a forecast is, but what it is worth for a choice such as postponing outdoor work, where a false alarm and a miss cost different amounts.

Radar, with a fair score

Rain fields scored with a neighbourhood score, so a storm forecast a few kilometres off is not punished twice.

What I am not claiming
  • A rain gauge is a point and a forecast is an average over a grid cell 9 to 28 km wide. Part of every low score is that mismatch.
  • The test is under three years, so each monsoon season appears two or three times.
  • The forecasts come from a public archive, and two faults were found in it. There may be others.
  • This is one region and one set of archives. It is not a ranking of models.

Method: the rules were written down before any forecast was scored, with a dated log of every change. Baselines are persistence (the observation 24 hours earlier) and climatology (station, month and hour, 2017 to 2023). Intervals: moving-block bootstrap over days, 8-day blocks, 2000 resamples, on paired differences.
