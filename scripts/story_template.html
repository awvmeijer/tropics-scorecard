How close was the forecast to what the instruments measured?

If a weather model is meant to learn from observations, it should be judged on them too. This page does that for Singapore: GFS and ECMWF forecasts scored against rain gauges, weather stations and airports, against two free baselines, with a 95 percent interval on every comparison. The ERA5 reanalysis is scored the same way, then used as the judge instead.

Anthony Meijer · November 2026 · Code · Full dashboard

In short
  1. Against the instruments, raw forecasts lose to a free guess. By root mean square error, GFS and ECMWF's IFS never beat climatology for temperature, wind or rain at a station, at any lead time. The only cells that do belong to ECMWF's AIFS, the learned model.
  2. The score and the judge decide the verdict. By mean absolute error ECMWF's rain looks 13 percent better than climatology, but an always-dry forecast looks 41 percent better. Judged by the ERA5 reanalysis instead of the instruments, GFS and ECMWF swap places for temperature.
  3. The gap is not just a point against a grid cell. A perfect forecast at ECMWF's grid size would cut 6-hour rain error at a gauge by 42 percent. ECMWF's cuts nothing, and its heaviest totals are a third of what the gauges measured.
  4. The information is there, and station data unlocks it. A plain linear fit of the same raw forecasts to 17 months of station data beats climatology for temperature by 13 percent on the following 16 months, and by 17 percent at stations it never saw.
01 · The gap at the surface

Against the instruments, the raw forecasts lose to a free guess.

Climatology is the forecast you get for free: what usually happens at this station, in this month, at this hour. The scorecard has the layout and the score, root mean square error, of the ones published for learned weather models, with one change: the truth is the instruments, not a reanalysis.

Part of the gap comes from comparing a point with a grid cell 9 to 28 km wide, partly over sea. But it is the gap a user at that point lives with, and the one a model trained on the observations themselves would have to close.

02 · Rain

For rain, the score you pick decides who wins.

My first version of this page said ECMWF's rain beat climatology by 12 percent. It did, by mean absolute error. Then I scored a forecast that never rains. Rain is zero most of the time, so mean absolute error rewards forecasting too little, and always-dry wins by a wide margin. A score that a useless forecast wins cannot be the headline.

Neither score is safe alone. One rewards dryness, the other smoothness, which is why sections 05 and 06 test the heavy rain and the decision directly. Reanalysis rain adds a third problem: it is itself a model estimate with known biases, the reason GraphCast's authors kept rain out of their main scorecard. A gauge has its own errors, but it measures the rain that fell.

03 · The ceiling

A perfect forecast at this grid size would do far better.

The usual defence of a low score at a gauge is that a point is not a grid cell. So I measured how much that costs. For each gauge I built the forecast a perfect 0.25° model would give: the true mean of the other gauges in its cell, with the gauge itself left out. It knows the cell's rain exactly and nothing finer.

How well two gauges agree on 6-hour rain, by the distance between them: the correlation of each pair, averaged by distance. Rain 2 km away tells you most of what fell here; rain 20 km away tells you about half as much.

04 · Temperature

The models miss the city's daytime heat.

Both models run cool, and ECMWF runs coolest between 9 in the morning and 3 in the afternoon. On its grid, the cell that holds about half the stations is mostly sea, and sea does not heat up during the day the way a city does. An average over all hours would hide this.

Mean error (forecast minus station) by local hour, one day ahead, in °C. Zero would be no bias. Shaded: 95 percent interval. Climatology sits 0.3 °C low because the test period was warmer than 2017 to 2023.

05 · Extremes

The heaviest rain is where the forecasts fall furthest short.

The FourCastNet paper tests extremes by comparing a model's heaviest values with the heaviest observed: the relative quantile error. Against reanalysis, its authors found ECMWF's heaviest rain about 15 percent too light. Against the gauges here, ECMWF's heaviest 6-hour totals are about two thirds too light.

A gauge is a point and the model gives a cell average, so I also averaged the gauges inside each cell. That closes only a small part of the gap.

Relative quantile error of 6-hour rain totals one day ahead, against the gauges averaged over each model's grid cell, at the 99th and 99.9th percentiles. Minus 50 percent means the model's heaviest totals are half what was measured; zero would be as heavy. Bars are 95 percent intervals. AIFS and IFS 9 km are on their shorter archives.

Three events show what this looks like. I chose them from the gauge data and public reports before looking at any forecast. The line marked Gauges is the network mean per hour; the others are what each model said one day ahead.

06 · The decision

Used for a decision, the forecasts keep little of a perfect forecast's value.

An error in millimetres says little about what a forecast is worth, so I scored a decision. You can protect against heavy rain at a cost, to avoid a bigger loss, and you protect whenever the forecast says 10 mm or more. A perfect forecast would save you a certain amount compared with the best fixed habit (always protect, or never). The share of that saving you keep is the forecast's value to you.

07 · How you judge

One matching choice moves the score more than switching models does.

A station has to be paired with one cell of the model's grid. Take the cell it sits in, or the nearest cell the model treats as land? For ECMWF the land rule moves 73 of the 74 stations in the island's southern cells to a cell centred in Johor, in another country.

I chose the cell the station sits in before seeing any score, and report the other as a check. A comparison that does not say which rule it used cannot be compared with anyone else's.

Every comparison on this page is made the same way: a model and a baseline are scored only on the cases where both have a value, and the difference is taken day by day before the interval, so neither side gets an easier set of days.

08 · The judge

Judge the same forecasts by the reanalysis, and the verdict changes.

ERA5 is the reanalysis most learned weather models are trained on and scored against. I used it two ways. First, I scored it against the instruments like any forecast:

Then I used it as the judge in place of the instruments, on exactly the same station-hours, with an ERA5 climatology from the same years as the free guess.

ERA5 does what it is built to do: estimate the weather over a 28 km cell. A model trained and scored against it learns to match that cell, not the instruments inside it.

09 · The usual statistics

Climatology passes the standard benchmarks. Most of the forecasts fail.

Regional model studies judge a simulation against stations with a fixed set of statistics and pass marks: for temperature, a mean bias within 0.5 °C and an index of agreement of at least 0.8; for wind, a bias within 0.5 m/s, a root mean square error of at most 2 m/s and an index of agreement of at least 0.6. 10 · A trained model

Fitted to the stations, the same forecasts beat climatology.

The raw forecasts lose to climatology, yet they follow the weather's departures from normal. So the information is there; it is just not expressed at the station. I fitted the plainest model that could use it: a linear regression on the station's climatology, the ECMWF and GFS forecasts, the time of day and year, and the station's position. It was fitted on the first 17 months of the test period and scored only on the 16 months after.

This is the case for training on observations, in miniature: the gain comes from the instruments, not from a bigger model. With under three years of data at one site, a deep network has nothing to add yet. What would add more is what a larger model can take in and this one cannot: radar, satellite and the stations around each station.

11 · The instruments

Before scoring a forecast, I had to trust the instruments.

A model trained on raw observations sees every fault in them unless something catches it first. These are the ones the checks caught in 202 million readings. Each flag is logged with the check that raised it, and the main scores are computed both with and without the flagged data.

Stuck gauges

1,850 gauge-hours

A gauge read zero while the gauges within 10 km had a median of 5 mm or more, the sign of a blocked funnel or a dead logger.

Frozen sensors

25,843 readings

Temperature and rain sensors repeating the same reading for an hour or more, caught as flatlines and removed.

Clocks and siting

5 station-months

The warmest hour of the day fell at 10:00 or 18:00 instead of early afternoon: a shifted clock or a badly placed sensor.

Different buckets

19 of 63 gauges

These gauges tip at 0.197 to 0.204 mm, not 0.2. The rain threshold sits at 0.1 mm, so a single tip counts on every gauge.

The feed itself

18,069 of 18,069

Before switching to a faster download endpoint, I compared it with the official one on a test day, reading by reading. Every shared rain reading matched.

A check against the record

18 of 18 days

The gauge data alone found Singapore's 2024 dry spell on exactly the dates in the official record.

12 · The archive

Two of my first results were faults in the archive.

On the first run, GFS rain looked better seven days ahead than one day ahead. That cannot be right, so I looked. Beyond 120 hours the archived GFS rain is a third of its day-one amount, and forecasting a third of the rain lowers the mean absolute error, the same trap as in section 02. I removed those lead times and wrote down why, even though that meant losing a result that looked good. The second fault ran the other way. ECMWF publishes rain in 3-hour and 6-hour steps, and the archive spreads each step over its hours, so hourly rain looked like drizzle that never fell. That is why rain is also scored in 6-hour totals.

A model trained on raw observations faces the same risk at scale: it can learn the quirks of an instrument or an archive instead of the weather. Checks like these have to run inside the pipeline.

Mean forecast rain per hour over Singapore, by how far ahead the forecast was made. A model's average rain should not depend on lead time. ECMWF's holds steady; GFS drops by two thirds after day 3.

13 · The harness

Built to be reused: any forecast plugs in the same way.

One interface

Anything that forecasts answers the same question: these stations, these times, these lead times. Persistence, climatology, four operational models, the ERA5 reanalysis and an always-dry forecast all use it, and a new source is one class.

One scorer

Every score, interval and comparison goes through one tested set of metric functions, so no two teams can compute "error" two different ways.

Rules before results

Matching, thresholds and baselines live in one config and a written protocol with a dated change log. The scorer refuses to run until the rules are signed off.

One command

One command rebuilds every table, score and chart from the raw downloads. 107 tests cover the metrics, matching and scorer.

14 · Next

What this judge would test next.

A model trained on observations

Scored at the same points, against the same baselines and intervals, and against the linear fit of section 10: the bar a learned model has to clear.

Ensembles, scored as distributions

Ensemble forecasts, such as GenCast's, scored at each station with the continuous ranked probability score and reliability diagrams, with a check that their spread matches their error. Both scores are already in the code.

Holdouts that have never been seen

Whole stations and whole instruments held out, not only later dates, so nothing in the test has reached training in any form.

Beyond the record

Error tracked against how far an event exceeds anything in the training years, where learned models have been shown to struggle.

Decisions with real costs

The value score in section 06 with a real user's costs, and with an ensemble's probabilities, so the choice can follow the chance of heavy rain instead of a yes or no.

Radar, with a fair score

Rain fields scored with a neighbourhood score, so a storm forecast a few kilometres off is not punished twice.

What I am not claiming
  • A rain gauge is a point and a forecast is an average over a grid cell 9 to 28 km wide. Part of every low score is that mismatch. For the heaviest rain, averaging the gauges over the cell closes only a small part of the gap.
  • The test is under three years, so each monsoon season appears two or three times. The trained model is scored on 16 months.
  • The forecasts come from a public archive, and two faults were found in it. There may be others.
  • This is one region and one set of archives. It is not a ranking of models.

Method: the rules were written down before any forecast was scored, with a dated log of every change. Baselines are persistence (the station's reading 24 hours earlier for each lead day, so a week earlier at day 7) and climatology (station, month and hour, 2017 to 2023). Intervals: moving-block bootstrap over days, 8-day blocks, 2000 resamples, on paired differences. The scorecard follows the layout of the GraphCast and GenCast papers, the extremes test is FourCastNet's relative quantile error, and the decision score is the relative economic value of the cost-loss model. These, the ERA5 comparison, the ceiling, the benchmark statistics and the trained model were added after the main scoring, and the protocol log says so. The benchmarks are those of Emery and others (2001).
